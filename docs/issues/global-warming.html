Global Warming

How is the Earth’s climate changing, what role do people play, and what should the country do about it?

What the issue is

Global warming means the Earth’s average temperature rising over many years. It is not the same thing as one hot afternoon, or one cold winter.

Sunlight warms the Earth. Some of that heat goes back to space. Gases in the air, including carbon dioxide, methane, and water vapor, hold some of the heat in. That is the greenhouse effect. It is part of why the planet is warm enough to live on. The argument is about the extra greenhouse gas people add, mostly by burning coal, oil, and natural gas, and by clearing forests.

Carbon dioxide is counted in parts per million: how many molecules of the gas are in a million molecules of dry air. At Mauna Loa, in Hawaii, the yearly average was about 316 parts per million in 1959 and about 427 in 2025.

A temperature number needs a baseline, the set of years a scientist calls normal. Two groups can agree on the warming and still print different degree numbers, because they picked different normals. This page labels every baseline.

  • The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change, in its 2023 summary of the science, says human activities, mainly greenhouse-gas emissions, have unequivocally caused global warming. Global surface temperature in 2011–2020 was 1.09°C above 1850–1900. The very likely range around that figure is 0.95°C to 1.20°C.
  • For the years from 1850–1900 to 2010–2019, the same summary’s best estimate of the human-caused warming is 1.07°C. Natural drivers, the sun and volcanoes, changed global temperature by about −0.1°C to +0.1°C. Well-mixed greenhouse gases likely warmed the surface by 1.0°C to 2.0°C. Other human effects, mainly tiny particles called aerosols, likely cooled it by 0°C to 0.8°C.
  • Those sentences are about the past. They are not a forecast of the year 2100.

Why people disagree

The amount of carbon dioxide in the air, and the rise in the measured temperature, are not the main fight. The fight is about two questions that often get mashed together: how much harm further warming will do, and what governments should require people and companies to do.

Future warming depends on how much greenhouse gas people add, and on how sensitive the climate is to that gas. Equilibrium climate sensitivity is the warming scientists expect, eventually, if the amount of carbon dioxide in the air doubles. The IPCC’s 2021 science report gives a best estimate of 3°C, a likely range of 2.5°C to 4°C, and a very likely range of 2°C to 5°C. A wide range means the same emissions can lead to different amounts of warming.

The IPCC also publishes projections. A projection is a careful estimate, not a record of something that has already happened. Compared with 1850–1900, the average temperature for 2081–2100 is very likely to be 1.0°C to 1.8°C on a very low emissions path, 2.1°C to 3.5°C on a middle path, and 3.3°C to 5.7°C on a very high path. In every path they studied, warming continues until at least the middle of the century unless emissions fall deeply.

One side hears those ranges and says the high end is a reason to cut emissions now. The other side hears the same ranges and says a law should not be written as if the hottest path were already a fact, because changing the energy system has a cost.

The strongest case for each side

Each column is the strongest fair case for that view. The charts in a column are ones that view usually cites. They are drawn from data files downloaded for this page. Read the lines under a chart: what it shows, and what it does not show.

Cut emissions, because the risk grows with the heat

This side starts with the measurements. The surface record rises, and the warmest years are the recent ones. Sea level measured by satellites is higher than it was in the 1990s. Late-summer Arctic ice covers less ocean than it did when those satellites began.

The IPCC says human-caused climate change is already affecting many weather and climate extremes in every region. Since the 1950s, heavy rain has become more frequent and more intense over most land where the records are good enough to judge, and human influence is likely the main driver. With each additional bit of warming, hot extremes and heavy rain get more intense. Global sea level has risen faster since 1900 than in any century in at least the last 3,000 years, the IPCC says, with high confidence. In 2011–2020, late-summer Arctic sea ice area was smaller than at any time in at least the past 1,000 years, with medium confidence.

On the charts above, September Arctic ice was 7.05 million square kilometers in 1979 and 4.81 in 2026. The lowest September in the file is 2012, at 3.57, so the ice has not fallen in a straight line. It is still far below the start of the record. From the 1993 satellite average to the 2024 average, global sea level on NOAA’s record rose 103 millimeters, about 10 centimeters.

This side says waiting makes the later cuts steeper if the world still wants one of the lower paths. It also says people who did the least to cause the emissions are often the most exposed to the heat, the rain, and the water.

The honest version of this view does not pretend that energy can be swapped out for free, or that one country’s law sets the whole world’s temperature. It says the cost of landing on a high warming path is the bigger risk, so it is worth paying to cut emissions now and to get ready for heat, floods, and higher seas.

Count the warming, and weigh the cost of the rules

This side does not have to deny the thermometer. The satellite record of the lower atmosphere rises too. From 1979 through 2025 it rises a bit more slowly than NOAA’s surface record. The two instruments are not looking at the same layer of air.

The IPCC’s own report says there is low confidence in long-term trends in the number of tropical cyclones of all strengths. It also says the share of major cyclones, category 3 to 5, has likely increased over about forty years. Drought is regional: human influence has contributed to more agricultural and ecological drought in some regions, and the report does not describe one worldwide drought trend of a single kind. A hotter planet is not the same sentence as “every kind of storm has become more common.”

Reported deaths from droughts, floods, storms, wildfires, and extreme temperatures are far below the famine decades of the early 1900s. They are not a straight slide, though. The 2020–2025 average is higher than the 2010s. Weather-disaster losses as a share of the world economy, in Roger Pielke Jr.’s 2018 series from Munich Re data, bounce between 0.10% and 0.48% of GDP from 1990 through 2017. They do not climb in a straight line, and the file stops in 2017.

World cereal yields rose from 1.35 tonnes per hectare in 1961 to 4.21 in 2024. A 2016 satellite study led by Zaichun Zhu, and described by NASA, found that from a quarter to a half of Earth’s vegetated land greened between 1982 and 2015. The researchers attributed about 70% of that greening to extra carbon dioxide and about 9% to nitrogen. They also said plants can adjust so the fertilizer effect fades, and that carbon dioxide still traps heat. This page does not chart that study, because the public write-up is a map and a set of shares, not a yearly table this site downloaded.

In 2024, fossil fuels were 82% of the energy used in the United States: petroleum 38%, natural gas 36%, and coal 8%. In 2022, U.S. energy-related carbon dioxide from fossil fuels was 4,941 million metric tons, out of a world total of 38,502 million in the same table, about 13%. A U.S. rule does not, by itself, set the world’s temperature.

The honest version of this view agrees that the air has more carbon dioxide, that the Earth has warmed, and that the IPCC finds people to be the main cause. The argument is that harm has to be counted in lives and costs, not only in degrees, and that replacing most of an energy system has a price. Money spent as if the hottest projection were already certain cannot also be spent on warnings, stronger buildings, or research.

Where they might agree

Both sides can use the same measurements. Carbon dioxide in the air is higher than it was in 1959. The surface and the lower atmosphere are both warmer than they were in 1979. A baseline has to be named, or two true temperature numbers look like a quarrel.

  • Projections are not promises. The IPCC’s very likely range for climate sensitivity, 2°C to 5°C, is wide on purpose.
  • A death toll and a temperature are different measurements. Fewer reported deaths do not erase the warming. The warming does not, by itself, tell you how many people will die.
  • High-confidence findings and low-confidence findings in the same IPCC report both belong in the story. Leaving one of them out changes it.
  • People need energy they can afford and count on. They also need protection from heat, floods, and higher water.

Questions to think about

These questions do not have an answer printed under them.

  1. NOAA’s surface record and the satellite record both rise from 1979 to 2025, at about 0.20°C and about 0.16°C per decade. What would you need to know before you said one of them was wrong?
  2. The IPCC’s very likely range for climate sensitivity is 2°C to 5°C. How should a law treat a range that wide?
  3. September Arctic ice was lowest in 2012, not in the latest year, and it is still well below 1979. What can one year prove, and what can it not prove?
  4. Reported deaths from weather and climate disasters are much lower than a century ago, and the 2020–2025 average is higher than the 2010s. What, besides the weather, can change a death toll?
  5. Disaster losses as a share of the world economy did not climb in a straight line from 1990 to 2017, and that file stops in 2017. What would you want to see after 2017 before you updated the conclusion?
  6. Extra carbon dioxide helps plants grow in the 2016 greening study, and it also traps heat. How should that change the way we talk about it?

Words to know

Greenhouse gas
A gas that holds heat in the atmosphere. Carbon dioxide, methane, and water vapor are greenhouse gases. The planet needs some of this effect. The argument is about the extra gas people add.
Parts per million
A way to count a rare gas. 427 parts per million of carbon dioxide means 427 molecules of that gas in every million molecules of dry air.
Baseline
The years a temperature number is compared with. NOAA’s surface chart on this page uses 1901–2000. A satellite chart may use 1991–2020. The warming can be the same when the printed degree number is not.
Projection
A careful estimate of the future, built from assumptions about emissions and about how the climate responds. It is not a record of what has already happened.
Climate sensitivity
How much the world is expected to warm, eventually, if the amount of carbon dioxide in the air doubles. The IPCC gives a best estimate and a range, not one certain number.
Emissions
Greenhouse gases released into the air. Burning coal, oil, and natural gas is the largest energy-related source. Clearing forests is another.
