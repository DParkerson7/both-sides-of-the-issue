About

Why this site exists

Division grows when people never hear the other side’s best argument, or never see the facts both arguments depend on. This site is a short, fair briefing for that problem.

The job of a page

Both Sides of the Issue explains divisive U.S. public questions in language a student in grades 6–8 can follow. Older students and adults who want a short briefing can use it too.

Each issue page has the same five parts: what the issue is, why people disagree, the strongest case for each side, where they might agree, and questions to think about. The parts are short on purpose. A small Words to know list and a Sources list sit underneath when a page is finished.

How a page stays fair

Fair here does not mean “both sides are equally right.” It means a thoughtful person on each side would recognize the case as their own, and that the facts under those cases are real.

  • We name a side by its idea, not by a political party.
  • We do not set up a weak argument just so the other side can win.
  • We do not use insults, sarcasm, or phrases like “the truth is” and “clearly.”
  • We do not invent a number to fill a hole. If a figure has not been checked in a source, it is left out.
  • We say when a number is a projection — a careful estimate of the future — rather than something that has already happened.
  • Two columns are a tool. If a third view matters, the page should say so.
  • We do not “balance” a documented fact with a slogan.

Where the facts come from

Government and other primary sources come first: laws, court opinions, and official statistics. On the Social Security page, that means the Social Security Administration and the Trustees who report on the program each year.

Just Facts, at justfacts.org, is a research library we use to find leads and to point readers toward a longer, footnoted page. It is not our publisher. We do not copy its wording, charts, tables, or the way it organizes a page. Each issue links the related Just Facts page as further reading.

This site is not Just Facts. It is not legal, tax, or investment advice.

What is ready, and what is an outline

The starter set has twelve issues. Social Security is the first full page. The other eleven are outlines. They are marked Coming soon, they keep the five-part structure, and they do not include statistics.

Some public issues need an older audience, or a slower pass through more than one kind of source, before a short middle-school page would be honest. They are not in this first set. Gun laws and immigration are here only as outlines so far. The full pages will stay age-appropriate. They will not use graphic stories, and they will not treat a contested claim as settled.

How to use the five buttons

On an issue page, choose a part with the buttons under the question. You can click them, or use the keyboard: Tab to the row of buttons, then the arrow keys to move. Home and End jump to the first and last part.

If a browser does not run JavaScript, the five parts appear one after another instead of behind buttons. Printing a page also shows every part.

When something is wrong

A wrong checked fact should be corrected in the content file, with the source saying what the number actually is. Cutting a sentence is better than keeping a number the source does not support.
